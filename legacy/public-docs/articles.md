## Article BG-EP01-v2

**Title:** NIL Reality Check for Parents: What to Ask Before a Deal  
**Suggested slug:** the-business-of-the-game/nil-reality-check-for-parents  
**SEO title:** NIL for Parents: Rules, Contracts and Money  
**Meta description:** Understand NIL deals, school revenue sharing and the questions sports families should ask about contracts, eligibility and taxes before signing.

### Article copy

A promising athlete. An interested business. An offer that sounds like an opportunity. Before a family says yes, the useful question is simple: What exactly are we agreeing to?

Name, image and likeness, or NIL, deals can compensate athletes for endorsements, promotional appearances and other uses of their identity. NCAA guidance describes third-party arrangements with defined promotional activity, a valid business purpose and reasonable compensation. Those requirements matter more than the excitement surrounding an offer. [NCAA NIL guidance](https://www.ncaa.org/student-athletes/name-image-likeness/)

Start by identifying who pays. A college’s direct revenue-sharing arrangement and a sponsorship from an outside business are separate agreements. The House settlement permits direct school payments in Division I while athletes can still enter private NIL agreements. A family should ask for each arrangement in writing and keep its obligations separate. [IRS explanation of the House settlement and NIL](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/name-image-and-likeness-income)

For Florida high-school families, begin with the FHSAA’s current NIL resources and your school’s athletic administration. FHSAA provides its amateurism bylaw, the GA1 affidavit and contract guidance. It also warns that meeting its requirements does not automatically satisfy the eligibility standards of a college association. Ask which forms, deadlines and restrictions apply before accepting an offer or creating sponsored content. [FHSAA NIL resources](https://fhsaa.com/sports/2024/8/12/ABOUT_NILResources.aspx?path=about)

Money is another part of the agreement that deserves attention. The IRS says NIL income is generally taxable, including noncash compensation such as merchandise or services. Reporting obligations can exist even when no tax form arrives. Keep records and ask a qualified tax professional how the particular arrangement affects your athlete. [IRS NIL income guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/name-image-and-likeness-income)

Our recommended parent checklist:

- Ask what the athlete must deliver, when it is due and when payment arrives.
- Identify who can use the athlete’s name, image and content, for how long and on which platforms.
- Ask about exclusivity, cancellation and any obligations after the relationship ends.
- Confirm school and association requirements, including permission to use uniforms or school branding.
- Have an independent attorney explain unclear terms before signing.
- Keep the athlete’s schoolwork, development and time commitments in the decision.

A useful offer should survive careful questions. Build the family’s decision around the written terms and the athlete’s goals, not a headline about someone else’s deal.

## Article BG-EP02-v2

**Title:** College Revenue Sharing: What Parents Should Ask Recruiters  
**Suggested slug:** the-business-of-the-game/revenue-sharing-era-explained  
**SEO title:** College Revenue Sharing: A Parent’s Guide  
**Meta description:** Learn how school revenue sharing differs from outside NIL deals and which written questions help families compare college offers.

### Article copy

When a college discusses money with a recruit, ask it to break the offer into separate parts: scholarship aid, direct school payments and any outside sponsorship arrangements. A single impressive total does not tell a family what is guaranteed, what must be earned or what could change.

The House settlement received final approval on June 6, 2025, creating the framework for participating Division I schools to share revenue directly with athletes. That opened a new part of the recruiting conversation alongside scholarships and other benefits. [NCAA statement on settlement approval](https://www.ncaa.org/news/media-center-a-letter-from-ncaa-president-charlie-baker/)

The annual school-level cap began at approximately $20.5 million for 2025–26. The College Sports Commission lists approximately $21.58 million for 2026–27. These are school-wide limits, not a promised payment to an individual recruit. A family needs the athlete’s actual written offer to evaluate what that program is proposing. [2025–26 framework](https://www.ncsl.org/resources/details/what-the-ncaa-settlement-means-for-colleges-and-state-legislatures) · [College Sports Commission revenue sharing](https://www.collegesportscommission.org/revenue-sharing/)

Outside NIL deals remain a distinct part of the picture. For example, an endorsement agreement with a business has its own payer and promotional obligations. NCAA guidance also sets reporting requirements for specified third-party deals through NIL Go. Ask the school’s compliance office which requirements apply to the athlete’s circumstances. [NCAA NIL guidance](https://www.ncaa.org/student-athletes/name-image-likeness/)

For the next recruiting conversation, we recommend asking:

1. What is the exact school-payment amount, and what period does it cover?
2. Which parts of the offer are guaranteed in writing, and which are estimates or opportunities?
3. What must the athlete do to receive each payment?
4. What happens under the agreement after injury, transfer, a coaching change or a roster decision?
5. Is scholarship aid documented separately, and who can explain its protections?
6. Who handles compliance questions, payment disputes and reporting?

Ask these questions of each school in the same format. Keep scholarship aid, school payments and third-party deals on separate lines in the family’s comparison. Do not count a possible endorsement as money available for tuition until its terms are established.

Avoid assuming the name of a payment settles its tax treatment. IRS guidance explains that tax obligations depend on the arrangement, including whether payment is for services or use of an athlete’s identity. Have a tax professional review the actual documents. [IRS NIL income guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/name-image-and-likeness-income)

The strongest recruiting comparison is one a family can explain without guessing: what the school offers, what the athlete owes in return, and what remains uncertain.

## Article BG-EP03-v2

**Title:** What Does a Full Ride Cover? Questions Every Sports Family Should Ask  
**Suggested slug:** the-business-of-the-game/real-math-of-a-scholarship  
**SEO title:** Full-Ride Scholarships: Costs and Questions  
**Meta description:** Compare scholarship coverage, award length, Division I protections and roster rules with a practical checklist for sports families.

### Article copy

“Full ride” sounds like the end of the money conversation. For a family comparing college offers, it should be the beginning of a detailed one.

Ask for the school’s written financial-aid offer and its itemized cost-of-attendance budget. Put them side by side. What is covered? For which months? What will the family still need to pay?

Athletics aid varies by school, sport and division. The NCAA explains that Division I schools can offer multiyear scholarships, so families should not assume every award lasts only one year. Read the agreement for its duration, amount and renewal terms. [NCAA scholarships and financial aid](https://www.ncaa.org/student-athletes/scholarships-and-financial-aid/)

Scholarship protections also matter. Under NCAA Division I core guarantees, an athlete who received athletics aid during the first year at that institution and still has eligibility remaining cannot have that aid reduced, canceled or not renewed for athletics-related reasons, including injury, performance or roster management. Schools must provide written notice and an appeal opportunity for an aid reduction, cancellation or nonrenewal. Ask the financial-aid and compliance offices how these protections apply to the specific offer. [NCAA core guarantees](https://www.ncaa.org/student-athletes/student-athlete-experience/core-guarantees/)

Roster rules have changed, too. At Division I schools subject to the House settlement framework, sport-specific scholarship limits were removed and roster limits introduced. The NCAA also adopted exceptions for designated athletes affected by the transition. Those athletes do not count toward roster limits for their remaining eligibility, including after a transfer. A roster limit alone therefore does not tell a recruit how many places or funded offers a school has available. [NCAA explanation of roster changes](https://www.ncaa.org/news/media-center-di-board-of-directors-formally-adopts-changes-to-roster-limits/)

Our recommended family worksheet has five parts:

- **Coverage:** List tuition, fees, housing, meals, books and other expected expenses. Ask what the award actually pays.
- **Timing:** Identify the months covered, payment dates and any summer costs.
- **Duration:** Record the award period and the written conditions for renewal or change.
- **Remaining cost:** Subtract confirmed grants and scholarships from the family’s expected expenses. List loans separately because they must be repaid.
- **Alternatives:** Compare another school where the academic fit and financial plan also work.

Ask the financial-aid office whether academic or need-based aid can be combined with the athletics award. NCAA Division I guidance permits combining aid up to the institution’s cost of attendance, but the office should calculate the actual package. [NCAA core guarantees](https://www.ncaa.org/student-athletes/student-athlete-experience/core-guarantees/)

Choose with the written offer in hand. The question is not simply whether a coach called it a full ride; it is whether the complete plan supports the athlete’s education and the family’s budget.
